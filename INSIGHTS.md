# Shift Log — every insight and every suggestion

Version 3.3.0. Two engines produce findings:

- **What your log suggests** — 15 personal readings, each ending in three
  suggestions. Only your own tickets, test tickets excluded.
- **Group insights** — 12 readings across the whole floor. These carry no
  suggestions, because nothing on that tab belongs to any one person.

Every reading has a **sample-size guard**: it does not appear at all until
there is enough behind it to mean something. On a small log you will see three
or four findings, not fifteen. That is the design, not a fault.

Wording marked *either / or* changes depending on which way the number runs —
the advice under a falling average is not the advice under a rising one.

# Part one — What your log suggests

## 1. Most popular

**Appears when** your most-logged party type has at least 3 tickets.
**Reads** which party type leads, on what share of your tickets, and what is
second.

- Lead with it when you write your own description — you are already known for
  it, and the ticket that matches the pitch is the one that closes.
- Offer the runner-up as the natural step up from it rather than a separate
  choice, so the second option is an addition and not a decision.
  *(If there is no runner-up: name a second option you would be happy to run,
  so there is somewhere to go when the first is not the fit.)*
- Check this against your best-paying type below — the party you are asked for
  most is not always the one worth steering toward.

## 2. Most profitable

**Appears when** a party type has at least 3 priced tickets and averages at
least 1.15× your overall average.
**Reads** which type earns most per party, and by what multiple.

- Raise what you quote on it first — it already carries your highest average,
  so it takes the increase most easily.
- Mention it early rather than waiting to be asked, while there is still room
  in the conversation to shape the booking.
- If a party is heading toward your overall average, ask whether it is on the
  table before settling the price.

## 3. Worth a look

**Appears when** your best-paying type is not your most-logged type, and comes
up less often.
**Reads** the money-per-party gap between the two, and the ticket counts.

- Bring it up in conversations drifting toward your most popular type — the
  gap per party is worth the sentence it takes to ask.
- Work out why it comes up less often: if it is not listed, not mentioned, or
  not priced clearly, that is a fixable reason rather than a lack of demand.
- Try leading with it for a week and watch whether the average moves — this is
  a small enough change to test cleanly.

## 4. Books best

**Appears when** two party types each have at least 4 decided tickets and
their book rates differ by at least 15 points.
**Reads** which type closes best against which closes worst.

- Steer toward the winner earlier in the conversation — it is where your yes
  rate is already highest.
- Look at what you say differently on the weaker ticket; the gap is more often
  in the framing than the party.
- When the weaker one looks unlikely to land, offer the stronger as an
  alternative rather than letting the conversation end.

## 5. Your guests

**Appears when** at least 5 tickets have guest history recorded.
**Reads** your split of new against returning, and how many tickets are
missing history.

- *Either* (60%+ new): you are living on new guests — a short message to past
  guests before a tour is the cheapest booking you will get.
  *Or:* your returning guests are carrying you — keep the list warm between
  tours, since a returning guest costs nothing to find.
- Ask every new guest one thing worth remembering and note it privately; a
  specific detail on the second visit is what turns one party into a habit.
- *Either:* tag guest history on the tickets missing it — this reading gets
  sharper the moment they are filled in.
  *Or:* keep tagging guest history on every ticket; it is what makes the
  repeat-value figures trustworthy.

## 6. New vs returning

**Appears when** both groups have at least 4 decided tickets and their book
rates differ by at least 10 points.
**Reads** which group books better, by how many points.

- *Either* (returning ahead): put your effort into the message after a party
  rather than the pitch before one — the second booking is where your rate is
  best.
  *Or:* your first meetings land; the drop is on the return, so treat the
  goodbye as the start of the next booking.
- *Either:* give returning guests a reason specific to them — a remembered
  preference beats a discount and costs nothing.
  *Or:* ask returning guests directly what would bring them back; a low return
  rate usually has one reason behind it.
- Track this again after the next tour — a gap this size is worth watching
  rather than acting on once.

## 7. Your week

**Appears when** you have at least 8 tickets across at least 3 different days
of the week.
**Reads** your busiest day, and your best-earning day when they differ.

- *Either* (they differ): hold your best availability for the best-earning day
  — the money is landing there, not on your busiest day.
  *Or:* that day is carrying both volume and money — protect it and do not
  fill it with low-value bookings.
- *Either:* quote a little higher on the best-earning day and see if it holds;
  a day that already earns more will bear it first.
  *Or:* try adding capacity around your busiest day, since the demand is
  proven there.
- Look at what makes your quietest day quiet — a slow day is either the wrong
  day or an unpromoted one, and those need different fixes.

## 8. Where it comes from

**Appears when** at least two ticket kinds (appointment, request, lineup, bar,
pull-in) each have 3 or more priced tickets.
**Reads** which source brings your highest average.

- Put more hours into whatever produces that source — it is your best-paying
  one per party.
- Work out what feeds it: if those come from a listing, a referral, or the
  floor, that is the thing to spend time on.
- Compare the effort each source costs you, not only what it pays — the
  highest average is only better if it does not take three times as long to
  get.

## 9. Your range

**Appears when** you have at least 8 priced parties.
**Reads** your 25th to 75th percentile — the middle half of your own prices —
and the midpoint.

- Open at your 75th percentile rather than your midpoint — a quarter of your
  parties already land at or above it, so it is a proven number, not an
  ambitious one.
- Treat your 25th as a floor and stop negotiating below it; the parties under
  it are the ones costing you the most per hour.
- Watch this range after any price change — the middle half moving up is the
  sign it held, and it moves before the average does.

## 10. Momentum

**Appears when** the tour in progress has at least 5 decided tickets, earlier
tours have at least 10 between them, and the book rates differ by at least 8
points.
**Reads** this tour's book rate against every earlier tour.

- *Either* (above): write down what you changed this run while it is fresh —
  the tour that works is only useful if you can repeat it.
  *Or:* list what is different this run — the town, the hours, the pricing,
  the mood. One of them is usually the whole gap.
- *Either:* this is the run to raise your quote on — a book rate this far
  above your usual means you have room.
  *Or:* before dropping your price, check the follow-through reading — a low
  rate is often arrivals rather than closing.
- Give it another week of tickets before you act on it — a tour can move eight
  points on a handful of nights.

## 11. Price trend

**Appears when** this tour has at least 3 priced parties, earlier tours at
least 5, and the trimmed averages differ by at least 12%.
**Reads** what a party is worth this tour against your history.

- *Either* (up): hold the new number. An average that has moved up is the
  price working, not a run of luck.
  *Or:* find where the drop is coming from — a lower average is usually one
  party type or one discount you started offering, not the whole tour.
- *Either:* raise your opening quote to match what you are already getting;
  the average moves after the ask does, not before.
  *Or:* stop quoting below your usual floor for the rest of this tour and
  watch whether the volume actually falls — often it does not.
- Check this against your book rate before deciding: a higher average with the
  same rate is a win, a higher average with a falling rate is a warning.

## 12. Follow-through

**Appears when** you have at least 8 marked appointments and at least 5
decided arrivals.
**Reads** appointments as a chain — how many arrive, then how many arrivals
close — and names which step loses more.

- *Either* (arrival is the leak): confirm the day before. Most appointments
  that never arrive were never really booked, and a reply the day before tells
  you which.
  *Or:* the people are turning up — the gap is in the room. Work on the first
  five minutes rather than on getting more appointments.
- *Either:* ask for something small at booking — a time confirmed in their own
  words, a deposit if you can take one. A guest who has committed to something
  turns up.
  *Or:* note the reason every time one walks; three or four entries usually
  show one repeated objection you can answer earlier.
- Fix the bigger leak first and leave the other alone — working on both at
  once makes it impossible to tell what helped.

## 13. Prime hours

**Appears when** you have at least 8 tickets across at least 3 hours with 3 or
more tickets each, and your busiest hour is not your best-paying hour.
**Reads** where the volume is against where the money is.

- Protect your best-paying hour. If you are tired or already booked by then,
  it is being spent on nothing.
- Try moving one booking a night from your busiest hour into it and watch what
  it does to the night's total.
- Quote a little higher in it — guests arriving in your best hour are already
  paying more than the rest.

## 14. Repeat value

**Appears when** new and returning guests each have at least 3 priced parties
and their trimmed averages differ by at least 20%.
**Reads** what a returning guest pays against a new one.

- *Either* (returning pay more): a returning guest is worth more than a new
  one — spend the time between tours keeping that list warm rather than only
  chasing new.
  *Or:* stop discounting the second visit. Familiarity is turning into a lower
  price, and that is a habit rather than a rule.
- *Either:* quote returning guests at the same level as new ones or higher;
  they already know what they are buying.
  *Or:* ask returning guests for the same party they booked first rather than
  assuming they want something smaller.
- Watch the pair of figures each tour — the gap between them tells you whether
  your repeat business is growing or eroding.

## 15. Streak

**Appears when** your last 3 or more decided tickets all booked.
**Reads** the run you are on right now.

- This is the moment to quote higher. Testing a bigger number costs least when
  the last several have all said yes.
- Write down what you are saying right now while it is still fresh — a streak
  is a script you have not written down yet.
- Do not read a streak as a new normal; keep an eye on your overall book rate,
  which moves slowly and lies less.

# Part two — Group insights

Across everyone's tickets, on the **Everyone** tab. No suggestions attached,
and nothing here can be traced to a person.

| Insight | What it reads |
|---|---|
| The usual range | The middle half of all shared prices, with the midpoint |
| Where the money is | The share of all booked money coming from the biggest tenth of parties |
| Most profitable | The segment taking a larger share of the money than of the tickets |
| Busiest against best paid | The hour with the most tickets against the hour with the highest average |
| The night that closes | The day of the week with the best book rate, against the worst |
| Where no-shows land | The day whose appointments least often arrive |
| Requests booked | How often walk-ins and call-ins convert across the floor |
| Repeat business | The floor's split of new against returning guests |
| Prices shared | How many bookings had a price shared, and how many were kept private |
| New guests | How new guests convert across the floor |
| Week over week | Last completed week against the one before — volume and book rate |
| The floor's weekend | The share of everyone's tickets landing Friday and Saturday |

# How to read all of this

Every figure comes from your own log. **What to try with it is a suggestion,
not an instruction** — the log sees numbers, not the night, not the guest and
not the room. A finding is worth checking against what you already know, and
when the two disagree, you are usually right and the log is usually missing
context.

Two habits make every reading sharper: tag guest history and party type on
each ticket, and mark attendance and outcome even when the answer is
disappointing. Most of the thresholds above are waiting on those fields rather
than on more tickets.

Hovering or tabbing onto any finding in the app shows exactly how that
particular number was worked out.

## New personal readings

| Reading | Appears when | Leads with |

| **Missing prices** | 5 or more bookings, 2 or more without a price | adding the price, privately if preferred |
